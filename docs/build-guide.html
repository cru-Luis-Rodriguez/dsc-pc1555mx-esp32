What you're actually building

Before the first step, it helps to know why the ESP32 is in the middle of this at all — it isn't a cable, it's a translator.

Before you start

Read first

If this panel is monitored, call the central station and put the account on test before you touch anything. Opening the can, cutting power, or a stray probe can trip an alarm and dispatch police to your house.

Never connect or disconnect a Keybus wire while the panel is powered. Kill the AC transformer and unplug the backup battery first, every single time. This is the one rule that protects both the panel and your board.

Finding the right resistors in that 130-value kit

The bags are labeled, so read the bag first. If a loose one gets away from you, the bands tell you what it is. Your kit is 1% metal film, which means five bands on a blue body.

The build

Nine stations. Do them in order — each one is arranged so that when something breaks, only the thing you just did can be responsible.

1MAC

Install the toolchain

No hardware yet. Get the software working while there's nothing physical to blame.

# PlatformIO builds and flashes the board
brew install platformio

# your project lives here
cd ~/Documents/projects/personal/security
ls

You should see platformio.ini, src/, include/ and docs/. If brew isn't found, install Homebrew first from brew.sh.

Checkpoint

pio --version prints a version number. That's all this step needs.

2MAC

Flash a naked board

Nothing is wired to the panel. This proves the Mac can talk to the ESP32 before any hardware is in play.

Plug one ESP32 into your Mac with a USB-C cable. Then find it:

ls /dev/cu.*

Look for /dev/cu.usbserial-0001 or similar. If nothing new appeared, the cable is charge-only — swap it. That's the single most common first-hour failure.

pio run -e serial -t upload -t monitor

First run downloads the ESP32 toolchain and takes a few minutes. Later runs take seconds.

Checkpoint

The monitor prints DSC PC1555MX Keybus interface starting... then Online. Waiting for Keybus data. and sits there. Silence after that is correct — nothing is connected yet.

Press Ctrl+C to exit the monitor.

If upload fails with a timeout, hold the BOOT button on the board while the upload starts, release once it says "Connecting..." Some boards need the nudge.

3BENCH

Build the divider board

The panel's Keybus swings to 12.6 V. Your ESP32's pins die above about 3.6 V. Two resistors per line fix that.

Solder a female header strip to the perfboard so the ESP32 plugs in rather than being soldered down permanently. You'll want that board back.

Checkpoint

With nothing connected to the panel, put your meter on resistance and probe from each junction to ground: 10 kΩ. Probe from each panel input to its junction: 33 kΩ. Probe from either panel input to ground: 43 kΩ. If any reading is wildly off, you have a solder bridge or a cold joint.

4PANEL

Wire into the Keybus

Four terminals, three wires for now. Power stays off the entire time.

Power down

Unplug the AC transformer at the wall and disconnect one battery lead inside the can. Confirm the keypad is dark before you touch a terminal.

The PC1555MX's Keybus is four screw terminals on the board, silkscreened RED, BLK, YEL, GRN — the same four your keypad already uses. You're tapping them in parallel, which the bus is designed for.

  • RED  Aux + 12 V — leave off until step 7
  • BLK  ground — connect first
  • YEL  clock → 33k → GPIO 18
  • GRN  data → 33k → GPIO 19

Connect black first. Ground is the reference the divider measures against; without it the other two readings mean nothing. Then yellow, then green. Leave red alone — for now your board draws power from the Mac's USB, which keeps the panel's power budget out of the equation while you debug.

Checkpoint

Three wires landed, red terminal untouched, no bare copper touching a neighboring terminal. Tug each wire gently — a screw terminal that lets go is a fault you'll chase for hours.

Now restore panel power: battery lead first, then AC.

5MAC

First light

This is the moment the project either works or doesn't. Everything after it is refinement.

pio run -e serial -t upload -t monitor

Checkpoint

Within a few seconds:

Keybus connected
Partition 1: Ready
Panel version: 32

Now walk to a door with a contact on it and open it. You should see Zone opened: 1 and, when you close it, Zone restored: 1. Note which zone number each door and window reports — you'll need that list in the next step.

If you see nothing, or a wall of garbage, jump to the troubleshooting table below. Don't proceed until this step is clean; every later problem gets ten times harder to diagnose on top of a flaky bus connection.

6MAC

Put it on the network

Same decoding, different output. Now it's a page anyone in the house can open.

cp include/secrets.h.example include/secrets.h
open -e include/secrets.h

Fill in your WiFi name and password. Then name your zones using the numbers you noted in step 5 — open src/web_status.cpp and edit the zoneNames list near the top:

const char* zoneNames[ZONE_COUNT] = {
  "Front Door", "Back Door", "Kitchen Window", "Garage",
  "Hallway Motion", "Living Room", "Zone 7", "Zone 8"
};
pio run -e web -t upload -t monitor

Checkpoint

The monitor prints an IP address. Open http://dsc.local/ in Safari — or that IP directly if mDNS is unreliable on your network. You get live arming state, every zone by name, AC power, battery, and the panel's own clock, refreshing every 1.5 seconds.

7PANEL

Cut the USB cord

Now the panel powers the board, so it keeps running through a blackout on the panel's battery — which is rather the point of a burglar alarm.

Set the voltage first

Set the buck converter to 5.0 V before it ever touches the ESP32. These modules ship at whatever the last person left them at, often 12 V or more, which will kill your board instantly. Feed its input from the panel's RED and BLK, put your meter on the output, turn the tiny brass screw until the display reads 5.0, then power down and connect the output.

Once it's set: panel power off, then RED → buck IN+, BLK → buck IN−, buck OUT+ → ESP32 5V (sometimes marked VIN), buck OUT− → ESP32 GND. Restore power.

Checkpoint

Unplug the USB cable entirely. The web page stays live. Mount the board and perfboard in the Zulkit box, run the four wires through a knockout, and close it up.

8KEYPAD

One change at the keypad

The panel ships with a setting that will quietly hide alarms from your new monitor.

Swinger shutdown stops the panel reporting a zone after three alarm events in one armed cycle. It exists so a faulty contact doesn't spam a monitoring service — but it blinds your interface too. Section [370] also holds a 30-minute delay before AC power loss is reported.

At your physical keypad, key:

[*] [8] [5555]        ← installer code, 5555 is the default
370
000 000 000 000 000
[#] [#]

That's the three swinger-shutdown counters and the transmission delay set to zero, then AC-failure reporting set to immediate. Press [#] to leave the remaining five entries alone.

Checkpoint

Trip a zone four times in one armed cycle. All four show up on your web page.

Full section reference — zone definitions, entry delays, attributes — is in docs/pc1555mx-programming.md.

9OPTIONAL

Talking back

Everything so far is read-only. One transistor turns the board into a keypad.

Writing to the Keybus means pulling the green data line low on cue, which the ESP32 can't do through a resistor divider. A 2N3904 does it: GPIO 21 → 1 kΩ → transistor base, emitter → GND, collector → the green line.

Then uncomment one line near the top of src/web_status.cpp:

// #define ENABLE_VIRTUAL_KEYPAD   ← delete the two slashes

Think about this one

The web page has no password. Enabling the keypad means anything on your network can disarm your house. Reasonable answers: leave it read-only, put the board on an IoT VLAN, or add HTTP basic auth. Deciding "later" is the same as choosing the first option, which is fine — just choose it on purpose.

Where to go next

Once this is running steadily for a week, the interesting doors are: Home Assistant via the library's MQTT examples; Dilbert66's ESPHome fork, which tracks the current ESP32 core and adds zone-expander emulation up to 64 zones plus a full LCD-style programming interface; or push notifications — the library ships working examples for Pushover, Telegram, and plain email.

Your local files: README.md for the toolchain notes and alternatives, docs/wiring.md for the parts list and divider details, docs/pc1555mx-programming.md for the complete installer-programming reference.
